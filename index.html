NAIRAS and the evidence boundary

MissionLab uses NAIRAS as related radiation-environment context, not as Artemis II crew dosimetry. NASA CCMC now documents NAIRAS Version 4 as an aerospace ionizing-radiation model spanning aircraft and spacecraft applications. MissionLab uses the NAIRAS family as environmental/model context, not as direct Artemis II personal dosimetry; crew-specific interpretation still depends on trajectory, vehicle geometry, shielding, detector response, and dosimetry assumptions.

Beyond Artemis II

Land on regolith without pretending Artemis II did.

Artemis II never landed on the Moon. This separate future-Artemis lab explores plume–surface interaction because the physics becomes critical for crewed lunar landing systems.

FUTURE ARTEMIS · EDUCATIONAL CONCEPTUAL MODEL

ARTEMIS II DID NOT LAND ON THE MOON.

NASA’s current architecture uses Artemis III as a 2027 low-Earth-orbit lander demonstration and targets Artemis IV for the first Artemis lunar landing in early 2028. Adjust the conceptual lander below to see why exhaust plumes can become a surface-operations hazard.

Cant = direction. Tilting an engine points the plume footprint sideways instead of directly below the lander.

Spread = width. A wider exhaust cone reaches more surface area and can enlarge the ejecta footprint.

WHY CANT MATTERS

It shifts the most energetic part of the plume footprint sideways, changing what nearby hardware may be exposed.

WHY SPREAD MATTERS

It changes how broad the surface loading and particle-ejection region can become.

Moderate conceptual ejecta footprintNearby hardware · watch exposureeducational model · not CFD, DSMC, or a validated landing prediction
Why PSI matters and what real analysis requires

Real PSI work needs actual engine/nozzle conditions, lander geometry, rarefied/continuum plume modeling, pressure and shear at the surface, regolith properties and particle-size distributions, erosion and entrainment physics, ejecta transport, nearby hardware geometry, and experimental or flight validation. NASA Langley is actively conducting vacuum-chamber PSI testing to reduce those risks for future lunar landers.
